Output scaling

When and why to change output coordinates

A surrogate may predict quantities with very different units, such as latency in seconds and energy in joules. Their numerical ranges can differ by several orders of magnitude. Plotting raw outputs can hide the smaller response, and an error metric that averages squared output errors can be dominated by the larger one. Explicit scaling makes the choice of relative units visible and can make comparisons and optimization gradients easier to interpret.

Core regression does not normalize automatically. fit defines residuals, ridge and smoothness in the output coordinates you provide. This is intentional: normalizing a response changes the meaning of zero, and therefore can change the prior expressed by a penalty. Choose units according to the prediction problem, retain the transformation, and invert it before reporting physical errors or sending predictions to downstream software.

Output scaling is separate from parameter coordinates. A simplex input row must still contain nonnegative barycentric values summing to one. Never apply an ordinary feature standardizer independently to those columns and then pass them to a Bézier space: the resulting rows will generally leave the domain. For a product domain, each barycentric factor has its own sum-to-one constraint.

A complete explicit transformation

This example standardizes two responses, fits the transformed observations, and restores predictions. Every tensor is float64 so that comparisons probe the transformation rather than float32 rounding of large offsets.

import torch
from torch_bsf import BezierSpace, Samples, fit
from torch_bsf.preprocessing import StdScaler

x = torch.linspace(0, 1, 21, dtype=torch.float64)
t = torch.stack([1 - x, x], dim=1)
values = torch.stack([x.square(), 1000 + 100 * x.square()], dim=1)
space = BezierSpace.simplex(1, degree=2)
scaler = StdScaler()
transformed = scaler.fit_transform(values)
scaled_result = fit(space, Samples(t, transformed)).require_success()
restored = scaler.inverse_transform(scaled_result.model(t))
direct_result = fit(space, Samples(t, values)).require_success()
assert torch.allclose(restored, direct_result.model(t), atol=1e-10)

The scaler’s statistics are fitted once to training outputs. Apply transform to later observations with those same statistics; do not refit them at prediction time. inverse_transform expects the same output-column order. If column names or units change, update the transformation and model together rather than silently reusing old statistics.

The model archive stores the learned model in its current coordinates. It does not package an arbitrary preprocessing object. Save the scaler’s numeric statistics and column schema through your application, and apply the inverse transformation when loading the model. A .bsf extension does not imply that normalization has been inferred or remembered.

Choose a scaler

Table 9 Available output transforms

Scaler

Transform

When to try it

MinMaxScaler

Subtract column minimum and divide by its training range.

Known bounded responses; a convenient nominal zero-to-one scale.

StdScaler

Subtract column mean and divide by its training standard deviation.

Compare variations relative to typical observed spread.

QuantileScaler

Use lower and upper quantiles as the reference range, without clipping.

Extreme observations would dominate a min-max scale.

NoneScaler

Identity transform and inverse.

Keep original units within a common preprocessing workflow.

Min-max scaling maps the training extrema to zero and one, but future observations may fall outside that interval. Standardization does not bound values at all. Quantile scaling is an affine change of units based on robust reference points, not a nonlinear transform to a uniform distribution. Values outside the selected quantile interval remain below zero or above one; they are not clipped or discarded.

QuantileScaler exposes its lower quantile fraction as q (default .05). Set it before fitting, for example scaler = QuantileScaler(); scaler.q = .1. Use 0 <= q < .5 so the reference interval is ordered and has a meaningful width. Increasing q ignores more tail observations when defining the reference range, usually reducing the divisor and making tails numerically larger after transformation. It does not reduce their regression weights. If robust fitting rather than robust scale estimation is required, this transform alone does not implement it.

Constant output columns use a divisor of one to avoid division by zero. StdScaler also guards its single-sample standard deviation. Near-constant columns can still have tiny nonzero ranges: check fitted statistics and decide whether amplifying their noise is useful. The supplied scalers estimate unweighted column statistics. Observation weights supplied to Samples do not automatically change those statistics; calculate an application-specific weighted transform when that is the intended meaning.

What changes mathematically?

For one output, write the transform as \(z=(y-a)/s\), with nonzero scale \(s\). Bernstein bases contain constants, so transforming each control \(Q=(P-a)/s\) gives the corresponding transformed polynomial. Without penalties or constraints, affine scaling leaves the exact least-squares predictions unchanged after inversion. It can change numerical rounding, but is not a mechanism for making an unregularized independent-column fit more expressive or statistically more accurate.

This observation also applies to multiple independent outputs: rescaling an output multiplies its data objective by a positive constant, without coupling it to other output columns. However, reported aggregate errors and acquisition scores do change their relative weighting across columns. Distinguish the model solution from the statistic used to rank competing models or queries.

With ridge, centering changes the preferred origin. Penalizing \(\|Q\|^2\) favors output \(a\) after inversion, whereas penalizing \(\|P\|^2\) favors original zero. Smoothness uses differences between neighboring controls and is invariant under translation. Pure multiplicative scaling of an independent output scales its data and quadratic penalty terms together, so it need not change its inverse-transformed optimum at unchanged penalty weights. Avoid assuming that every change of units automatically requires a different fitted curve. Check the exact transform, objective and cross-output selection criterion.

Fixed values must be transformed by the same affine rule as observations. If a known endpoint is 1000 in original units, fixing it to 1000 inside a standardized fit imposes an entirely different constraint. Transform its one-row output tensor, then create FixedControls from that transformed value. Invert predictions before checking agreement with physical boundary conditions. See Fixed controls for resolving coefficient indices.

The comparison uses a cubic model and the same noisy observations in both outputs, with the second output translated and scaled. Reproduce it with python examples/generate_guide_figures.py; the maximum inverse-prediction difference is recorded in metrics.json.

Validation without leakage

Fit preprocessing on each training fold, transform that fold’s held-out outputs with the training statistics, predict, invert, and evaluate in the units that matter. Fitting the scaler on all outputs before splitting uses test information. For a regularized fit, that can also change its preferred origin using held-out data. The convenience cross_validate function does not fit preprocessing objects for you.

A practical manual fold loop uses KFold.split(len(samples)) and samples.take(indices). Create a fresh scaler in each iteration. Keep original held-out values for reporting error, and use the same fold partitions for every degree and penalty comparison. If you select candidates by standardized error, explain that each output is weighted relative to its training spread; if you select by original-unit error, report the units and output-specific tolerances.

After selection, refit the scaler on all available training data and train a fresh model with the chosen settings. Keep a final untouched test set if one is needed. See Cross-validation and degree search for metrics and Explicit ensembles and active learning for how changing output scales changes committee disagreement rankings.

Extreme finite values

Fitting requires nonempty finite input tables. After fitting, transform and inverse_transform require a two-dimensional table with the same number of features as the fitted data, raising ValueError for other shapes rather than broadcasting columns. The number of rows may differ, including a batch of zero rows with shape (0, n_features). For example, after fitting two columns, scaler.transform(torch.ones(3, 1)) raises ValueError; provide both columns in their original order. Refitting establishes a new feature count.

StdScaler and QuantileScaler require floating values; MinMaxScaler also accepts integral tensors. For floating input, statistics and transformed outputs must fit the input dtype. Standard deviations and quantiles retry with scaled arithmetic when ordinary reductions overflow; constant and single-sample columns retain their unit-scale convention. Transforms and inverse transforms also retry a factored affine expression when a subtraction or product overflows before cancellation.

The fitted public scales / stds attributes remain single tensors in the original units. If a range or sample standard deviation itself exceeds that dtype, fitting raises ValueError with a recommendation to rescale the original units. For example, float64 [-1e308, 1e308] has an unrepresentable min-max range (2e308), although its sample standard deviation is representable. The min-max and default quantile scalers reject this range explicitly; the standard scaler returns approximately [-0.7071, 0.7071]. Divide the original data by a suitable unit (for example 1e308) before fitting when needed, and retain that unit separately for interpreting inverse transforms.

Non-finite input and genuinely unrepresentable forward or inverse results also raise ValueError instead of propagating NaN/Inf. A successful inverse is still subject to floating rounding: a spread smaller than the precision of a large offset may already have been lost in the supplied data. These scalers do not recover information absent from the input dtype. NoneScaler remains a pass-through, without numerical validation.

A varying column whose nonzero standard deviation rounds to zero also raises ValueError. The unit-scale convention applies to genuinely constant columns and single samples. Scale tiny original units upward before fitting; otherwise substituting a unit standard deviation would silently skip standardization of varying data.

For integral MinMaxScaler input, fitted minima and ranges retain their integer dtype; a range exceeding that dtype raises ValueError before subtraction can wrap. Subtraction happens before true division, preserving adjacent integer differences even at large offsets. Normalized values follow PyTorch’s default floating dtype; half outputs use float32 division before casting to that dtype. A remaining nonfinite integer transform uses exact rational CPU arithmetic before rounding once to the output dtype; this preserves the last finite half rounding interval. Floating inverse results have floating precision, so adjacent large integers can round together in that output dtype. Boolean and complex inputs remain unsupported. Integer extrapolation retries a floating expression when subtraction would wrap. Integer inputs to inverse_transform keep their promoted integer output dtype: risky multiplication or addition uses exact Python integers on the CPU, and a final result outside that dtype raises ValueError. Ordinary integer inverse operations stay on the tensor device.
